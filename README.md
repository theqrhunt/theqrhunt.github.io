What is The Qr Hunt?

The Qr Hunt started with a very simple idea:

Take one physical card.

Put a QR code on it.

Write something like “SCAN ME!” on it.

Leave it somewhere.

Wait.

That’s basically it.

Except it isn’t.

The whole point of the project is for that one physical QR code card to travel.

It might be found by someone sitting in a café. Someone walking through a park. Someone waiting for a bus. Someone bored at school. Someone who has absolutely no idea what they’re looking at.

They scan it.

They arrive at the website.

And the website tells them:

Thanks for scanning.

You found it.

Now become part of it.

They enter a nickname.

Their name appears on the chain.

And then comes the important part.

They take the card and leave it somewhere else.

And just like that, the hunt continues.

⸻

Why?

Honestly?

There isn’t a particularly important reason.

And that’s kind of the point.

The internet is full of enormous things.

Social networks.

Algorithms.

Advertisements.

News.

Notifications.

Metrics.

Followers.

Likes.

Comments.

Subscriptions.

Artificially optimized attention.

Everything seems to be trying to accomplish something.

The Qr Hunt doesn’t really need to.

It is just a tiny experiment in what happens when you put a small mysterious object into the real world and ask strangers to participate.

There is no prize.

There is no grand competition.

There isn’t even a winner.

There is just a QR code.

And whoever happens to find it.

“Sometimes the most interesting things are the things that don’t need a reason to exist.”

Maybe someone finds it and thinks it’s stupid.

Fair enough.

Maybe someone scans it and immediately closes the website.

Also fair.

Maybe someone scans it, puts their nickname on the list, and leaves it somewhere else.

That’s the person we’re looking for.

⸻

The Chain

The website keeps a list of everyone who has scanned the QR code.

The first person is The Origin.

They are the beginning.

After that, everyone becomes another link in the chain.

One person becomes two.

Two become ten.

Ten become fifty.

And fifty become a number that starts looking slightly ridiculous.

Maybe it stays small.

Maybe twenty people find it.

Maybe one hundred.

Maybe one thousand.

Maybe nobody cares and the QR code ends up underneath a bush somewhere.

That is also a perfectly valid ending.

But there is something fascinating about not knowing.

Because the QR code is a physical object.

The website can tell us that someone scanned it.

It can tell us their nickname.

It can tell us approximately where they were, if they choose to share their city.

But it can’t tell us what happened between the scans.

That’s the part that belongs to the real world.

Someone has to physically pick it up.

Someone has to decide where to leave it.

Someone else has to notice it.

And suddenly two complete strangers are connected by something neither of them planned.

⸻

The Journey

At the beginning, the QR code might only travel a few hundred meters.

Maybe it starts in one neighborhood.

Then someone takes it across the city.

Then someone else finds it.

Then someone goes on a trip.

And suddenly:

The QR code is in another city.

Then another person travels.

And suddenly:

It’s in another country.

And now things get interesting.

Imagine looking at the website one day and seeing:

47 people

12 cities

4 countries

1 very confused QR code

That would be pretty funny.

But it would also be kind of amazing.

Because nobody would have planned it.

There wouldn’t be a central organizer moving the card around.

The people themselves would be moving it.

The project would essentially be running on curiosity.

The Weird Thing About It

There is something strangely beautiful about the fact that the QR code has no idea where it is going.

Neither do I.

That’s one of my favorite parts of the project.

Once the card is released, I don’t really control it anymore.

I can update the website.

I can look at the list.

I can watch the numbers increase.

I can wonder where the card currently is.

But I cannot physically follow it.

At some point, it becomes its own little traveler.

And maybe that’s what makes the project interesting.

You create something.

You let it go.

And then you see what other people do with it.

“A journey is sometimes more interesting when you don’t know the destination.”

The QR code has a destination.

We just don’t know what it is.

The Numbers

The numbers on the website aren’t really supposed to be a leaderboard.

There is nothing to compete over.

You don’t get more points for finding it first.

You don’t unlock a skin.

There isn’t a suspicious little QR Hunt cryptocurrency waiting for you.

Please don’t ask.

The number simply represents your place in the story.

#001 means you were there at the beginning.

#100 means ninety-nine people were there before you.

#1,000 means something has gone very, very right.

And if we ever reach #10,000, I think we have officially lost control of the experiment.

At that point the README will probably need to be rewritten.

What I Hope Happens

I don’t necessarily want millions of scans.

That would be cool, obviously.

But I think a smaller chain that genuinely travels is more interesting than a giant number created by people scanning it once and forgetting about it.

I’d rather see:

100 people across 20 cities

than:

10,000 people who all scanned it in the same place.

Because the real goal isn’t the number.

It’s the journey.

I want the physical card to move.

I want people to wonder where it has been.

I want someone to find it somewhere completely unexpected.

I want someone to scan it at 2 AM and wonder why there is a QR code sitting there.

I want someone to find it months after the previous person left it.

I want someone to take it to another city.

And, ideally, I want someone to take it to another country.

And Maybe It Doesn’t

There is also a possibility that none of this happens.

Maybe the first few people scan it.

Someone forgets to move it.

It rains.

The paper gets destroyed.

Someone throws it away.

The chain ends.

And that’s okay too.

Because the experiment was never really about guaranteeing an outcome.

It was about creating the possibility of one.

The same way you can throw a message in a bottle into the sea without knowing whether anyone will ever find it.

The difference is that this bottle has a QR code.

And JavaScript.

Much more technologically advanced.

A Tiny Social Experiment

In a weird way, The Qr Hunt is a tiny social experiment.

Not a serious scientific experiment.

Please do not cite this README in an academic paper.

But it asks a simple question:

Will strangers continue something that they did not start?

I put the first card somewhere.

One person finds it.

I ask them to move it.

They ask another person to continue.

That person asks another.

And so on.

At some point, the project no longer belongs to the person who created it.

It belongs to everyone who participated.

That’s the part I find interesting.

The Rule

There is really only one rule:

If you find it, move it.

That’s it.

Don’t destroy it.

Don’t keep it forever.

Don’t put it somewhere impossible to find.

And please don’t put it somewhere dangerous.

The goal is to give another person the chance to discover it.

Because the person after you is the reason your scan matters.

You aren’t the end of the chain.

You are a link.
